# Clarity Is the New Uptime

*A month of disruptions, from software bugs to a supplier breach to a ransomware crew's boasts, was a reminder that going dark is a shared condition of modern infrastructure, not a mark against any one institution. The more interesting questions are what a disruption actually tests, and what regulators are about to require.*

*Draft for the TransPerfect Financial Services blog · 2026-11-19*

---

This month delivered a run of disruptions with almost nothing in common but their timing: a major cloud-productivity platform, an aviation system, payments wobbles at [several large banks](https://www.newsweek.com/major-banks-down-america-everything-broken-microsoft-faa-outages-12723495), and, days later, a [ransomware group's unverified claims](https://www.techradar.com/pro/security/visa-confirms-apparent-cyberattack-hackers-also-claim-hit-on-jpmorgan) against some of the biggest names in cards and banking. The causes ran the full range. At least one outage traced to an [ordinary software bug rather than a cyberattack](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/16/business/bank-outage-chase-us-bank-bank-of-america); one card network said its own systems were untouched and pointed to a third-party supplier; another large institution called its disruption a bug with no sign of an attack.

That spread is the real lesson. Disruption now arrives from every direction, your own code, a supplier three steps removed, or someone actively trying to break in, and in an economy this interconnected, no institution is immune. Most, as this month showed, recover quickly. So the useful question is not how to never have an incident. It is what an incident actually tests.

Here is the part that tends to get overlooked. When everything is working, an institution's product is its technology: the app, the rails, the instant transfer. When something breaks, all of that is, by definition, unavailable, and the only product the institution can still deliver is communication, telling people what is happening, what to do, and when it will be back. A disruption quietly flips the value proposition from engineering to language.

And communication is hardest at the exact moment it matters most. Volume spikes instantly, uncertainty runs high, and the audience is the entire customer base at once, which for any large institution today means many languages, not one. Technical resilience has matured into a well-funded discipline of failover, redundancy, and recovery. The communication layer of resilience, clear and calm and in every customer's language, across channels, at surge volume, is the part that is still mostly improvised.

## The rules are catching up

Regulators are moving in the same direction, and faster than most realize. US banking agencies already require an institution to [notify its primary regulator within 36 hours](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2021/bulletin-2021-55.html) of a significant computer-security incident, and that same rule obliges third-party service providers to alert the banks they serve, the exact supplier-failure scenario that surfaced this month. Public companies must disclose a material cyber incident to the SEC [within four business days](https://incidentcost.com/industries/financial-services). In the UK, the FCA, PRA, and Bank of England have finalized [operational-incident and third-party reporting rules](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/prudential-regulation/publication/2026/march/operational-incident-and-third-party-reporting-policy-statement) that firms must meet by March 2027, with initial reports due [within 24 hours, or four for payment providers](https://www.tlt.com/insights-and-events/insight/new-operational-incident-reporting-rules-for-banks-and-crr-firms-what-you-need-to-do-before-march-2027), and a fresh consultation on cyber and outage readiness already underway. The EU's DORA framework points the same way.

Two things stand out. The clocks are tightening, and the scope now explicitly covers outages, third-party failures, and the attacks behind them, not just classic data breaches. But the deeper shift is in what regulators are measuring: operational resilience is increasingly judged by customer outcomes and harm, not only by how fast a system comes back. That is precisely where communication lives. The duty to tell people what is happening, clearly and in the languages they actually use, is moving from good practice toward expectation.

Everyone goes down. What a customer remembers afterward is rarely the length of the downtime. It is whether, in those few minutes, someone told them what was happening, clearly, and in a language they understood. As resilience matures, and as the rules around it harden, that may turn out to be the most human part of it.
